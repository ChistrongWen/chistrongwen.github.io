01 · Stress, strain and deformation

Distinguish displacement from strain

A displacement vector \(\mathbf u\) describes the motion of a material point. Strain describes spatial changes in displacement, not displacement itself: a rigid translation can have nonzero displacement and zero strain. The small-strain tensor is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient; its antisymmetric part describes infinitesimal rotation.

\[\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac12\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right),\qquad \varepsilon_v=\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}.\]

Normal strain is fractional length change; engineering shear strain is \(\gamma_{xy}=2\varepsilon_{xy}\). Stress is force per unit area, measured in pascals. In continuum mechanics the traction on a plane with unit normal \(\mathbf n\) is \(\mathbf t=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf n\). Here tensile normal stress and extension are positive; hydrostatic pressure is positive in compression.

\[\sigma=\frac{F}{A},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\Delta L}{L_0},\qquad p=-\frac13\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\sigma}.\]

02 · Linear elasticity and elastic constants

Hooke's law in three dimensions

A linear elastic solid responds instantaneously and reversibly to stress. Isotropic elasticity requires two independent elastic constants. Young's modulus \(E\) measures axial stiffness in a uniaxial stress test; Poisson ratio \(\nu\) relates lateral strain to axial strain. Shear modulus \(\mu\) measures resistance to shape change; bulk modulus \(K\) measures resistance to volume change. The first Lamé parameter \(\lambda_L\) is not an independent third constant.

\[\boxed{\boldsymbol{\sigma}=2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}+\lambda_L\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\mathbf I}\]\[\boldsymbol{\sigma}=2\mu\mathbf e+K\varepsilon_v\mathbf I,\qquad \mathbf e=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\frac{\varepsilon_v}{3}\mathbf I.\]
\[\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},\qquad K=\frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)},\qquad \lambda_L=K-\frac23\mu.\]\[E=\frac{9K\mu}{3K+\mu},\qquad \nu=\frac{3K-2\mu}{2(3K+\mu)}.\]

For stable isotropic elasticity, \(K>0\), \(\mu>0\), and \(-1<\nu<1/2\). As \(\nu\) approaches 1/2 at fixed \(E\), the bulk modulus diverges: the incompressible limit. A large Poisson ratio is not by itself evidence for low viscosity.

Energy stored in elastic deformation

\[W=\frac12\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\mu\mathbf e:\mathbf e+\frac12K\varepsilon_v^2.\]

For an ideal elastic material, this energy is recovered upon unloading. A viscous material dissipates energy instead. This distinction underpins the transition from instantaneous deformation to time-dependent rheology.

Elastic surface loading: point mass response

\[u_z(r)=-\frac{(1-\nu^2)g\Delta M}{\pi Er}.\]

For a homogeneous isotropic half-space, the Boussinesq surface response is inversely proportional to distance and Young's modulus. Positive mass gives negative vertical displacement. This local approximation does not include spherical geometry, layering or self-gravity. Do not evaluate a point-load kernel at zero distance; integrate finite load areas instead.

03 · From laboratory elastic constants to spherical Earth models

Seismic velocities and elastic properties

For an isotropic elastic solid, the P-wave and S-wave velocities are determined by density and elastic moduli. A layered Earth model specifies radial functions of density and stiffness instead of using a single homogeneous modulus.

\[V_P=\sqrt{\frac{K+4\mu/3}{\rho}},\qquad V_S=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}}.\]\[\mu=\rho V_S^2,\qquad K=\rho\left(V_P^2-\frac43V_S^2\right),\qquad \nu=\frac{V_P^2-2V_S^2}{2(V_P^2-V_S^2)}.\]

How elastic structure enters load Love numbers

\[\{\rho(r),K(r),\mu(r)\}\ \longrightarrow\ \{h_n',l_n',k_n'\}\ \longrightarrow\ G_u(\psi).\]\[G_u(\psi)=\frac{a}{M_\oplus}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h_n'P_n(\cos\psi).\]

The spherical self-gravitating elastic boundary-value problem combines force equilibrium, mass conservation, gravitational potential perturbations and interface/surface conditions. Once load Love numbers are calculated, their response already incorporates the chosen Earth structure; do not multiply the resulting Green function by an additional factor of Young's modulus or Poisson ratio.

Elastic load Love numbers differ from tidal Love numbers. Degree-one reference conventions (CM, CF or other choices) must be consistent with GNSS coordinates. High-degree convergence and finite-cell integration are critical for local loads.

04 · Viscosity and material time scales

Newtonian viscous deformation

A Newtonian dashpot relates shear stress to shear strain rate. Under constant stress it continues deforming; the strain does not recover when stress is removed. Viscosity is measured in Pa s, not Pa. Mantle viscosity may depend on temperature, pressure, water content, grain size and stress. In nonlinear creep, effective viscosity depends on the deformation state.

\[\tau=\eta\dot\gamma,\qquad \gamma(t)=\frac{\tau_0}{\eta}t.\]

For deviatoric tensor components the Newtonian relation is \(\mathbf s=2\eta\dot{\mathbf e}\); the factor of two reflects tensor shear strain versus engineering shear strain. The experiments below use scalar engineering shear stress and strain throughout.

\[\tau_M=\frac{\eta}{\mu},\qquad \mathrm{De}=\frac{\tau_M}{T}.\]

The Maxwell material time compares viscous and elastic behavior. A Deborah number much greater than one suggests elastic behavior over the observation duration \(T\); a small value suggests substantial relaxation. A material Maxwell time is not necessarily the relaxation time of a loaded, layered, self-gravitating Earth.

05 · Classical rheological models

Mechanical analogues

Springs store elastic energy and dashpots dissipate energy. In series, stress is shared and strains add; in parallel, strain is shared and stresses add. These elementary rules generate several useful linear rheologies.

Creep under a step stress

For a stress step \(\tau_0 H(t)\), the creep compliance \(J(t)=\gamma(t)/\tau_0\) describes the time evolution of strain. Let subscripts M and K denote the Maxwell and Kelvin elements.

\[\begin{aligned}J_{\mathrm E}(t)&=\frac1{\mu_M},\\J_{\mathrm M}(t)&=\frac1{\mu_M}+\frac{t}{\eta_M},\\J_{\mathrm{KV}}(t)&=\frac1{\mu_K}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau_K}\right),\\J_{\mathrm B}(t)&=\frac1{\mu_M}+\frac{t}{\eta_M}+\frac1{\mu_K}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau_K}\right),\quad \tau_K=\eta_K/\mu_K.\end{aligned}\]

Stress relaxation under a step strain

For a strain step \(\gamma_0 H(t)\), the relaxation modulus \(G(t)=\tau(t)/\gamma_0\) describes stress decay. The ideal Kelvin–Voigt model produces an impulsive stress at the instant of an infinitely fast strain step; its finite stress for \(t>0\) equals \(\mu_K\gamma_0\). For the Burgers model, the relaxation function contains two exponential modes.

\[G_{\mathrm E}(t)=\mu_M,\qquad G_{\mathrm M}(t)=\mu_M e^{-t/\tau_M},\qquad G_{\mathrm{KV}}(t)=\mu_K\quad(t>0).\]

Standard linear solid (Zener model)

A spring of modulus \(\mu_\infty\) in parallel with a Maxwell element of modulus \(\mu_1\) and viscosity \(\eta_1\) gives a finite equilibrium stiffness, unlike a single Maxwell element. The relaxation modulus is \(G(t)=\mu_\infty+\mu_1e^{-t\mu_1/\eta_1}\). Generalized Maxwell models use several relaxation modes. They are useful for representing a spectrum of time scales.

06 · Periodic forcing and frequency-dependent response

Complex shear modulus

Under harmonic strain \(\gamma(t)=\Re[\gamma_0e^{i\omega t}]\), a linear viscoelastic material responds with a complex modulus \(G^*=G'+iG''\). The storage modulus \(G'\) characterizes reversible energy storage; the loss modulus \(G''\) characterizes dissipation. The stress leads the imposed strain by phase angle \(\delta=\arg G^*\).

\[G^*_{\mathrm M}=\mu\frac{i\omega\tau_M}{1+i\omega\tau_M},\qquad G'=\mu\frac{(\omega\tau_M)^2}{1+(\omega\tau_M)^2},\qquad G''=\mu\frac{\omega\tau_M}{1+(\omega\tau_M)^2}.\]\[G^*_{\mathrm{KV}}=\mu+i\omega\eta,\qquad Q^{-1}_{\rm loss}=\tan\delta=\frac{G''}{G'}.\]

07 · Loading history and delayed deformation

From elastic spatial convolution to viscoelastic space-time convolution

An elastic load response depends only on the current mass anomaly relative to its reference state. A linear viscoelastic response also depends on when the load changed. Define \(G_u(\psi,t)\) as the displacement step response to unit mass added at time zero.

\[u(\mathbf x,t)=\int_S\int_{-\infty}^{t}G_u(\psi,t-t')\,\frac{\partial\sigma(\mathbf x',t')}{\partial t'}\,dt'\,dA'.\]

This form uses load increments and a step-response kernel. An impulse-response formulation instead convolves the mass history itself with the time derivative of the step kernel (including its instantaneous contribution). Do not confuse the two kernels. A discretized history uses mass increments \(\Delta M_{jk}\) at epochs \(t_k\).

\[u_i(t)=\sum_j\sum_{t_k\le t}G_u(\psi_{ij},t-t_k)\Delta M_{jk}.\]

08 · Earth deformation and geodetic applications

Elastic lithosphere and viscoelastic mantle

On many geodetic time scales the lithosphere is approximated as elastic while deeper mantle layers may relax. The elastic thickness is an effective mechanical parameter, not automatically identical to the crustal thickness or thermal lithosphere thickness. A thin elastic plate approximation has flexural rigidity

\[D=\frac{ET_e^3}{12(1-\nu^2)}.\]

Changing elastic thickness alters spatial patterns; changing viscosity alters temporal evolution. Both can affect the observed uplift rate. A full spherical viscoelastic model uses radial density, elastic moduli, rheology, gravitational coupling and the complete load history. Its relaxation modes depend on harmonic degree and Earth structure.

09 · Observations and inverse problems

GNSS measures three-dimensional motion at stations, InSAR measures relative line-of-sight deformation, GRACE measures gravity changes, and altimetry measures surface elevation. Combining these observations can constrain forcing and rheology but requires a consistent reference frame, spatial sampling, observation operator and covariance model.

\[\mathbf y=\mathcal H[\mathbf m(t),\boldsymbol\theta]+\boldsymbol\epsilon,\qquad \boldsymbol\theta=(T_e,\eta_1,\eta_2,\ldots).\]\[\Phi=(\mathbf y-\mathbf y_{\rm model})^T\mathbf C^{-1}(\mathbf y-\mathbf y_{\rm model})+\alpha\mathcal R(\boldsymbol\theta).\]
  1. Reconstruct loading or earthquake forcing over the required historical time span.
  2. Calculate instantaneous elastic and time-dependent responses for candidate Earth structures.
  3. Project model displacement into GNSS or InSAR observation geometry; for gravity, calculate the corresponding gravitational response.
  4. Compare data and predictions after consistent spatial and temporal referencing.
  5. Assess uncertainty, parameter trade-offs, forcing uncertainty and independent validation.

Research example: Kerguelen Island

A possible study would combine ice-mass histories from satellite gravimetry and altimetry with GNSS and InSAR measurements on stable bedrock. An observed excess uplift relative to an elastic prediction could motivate a viscoelastic hypothesis, but tectonic, volcanic, reference-frame and load-history errors must be evaluated. Low effective viscosity alone does not establish hotspot temperature, water weakening or partial melt.

Key distinction: viscosity is a material parameter; elastic thickness is a structural parameter; Maxwell time is a constitutive time scale; observed surface relaxation time is a system response.
