01 · Measurement principles

Time of flight and precise orbit determination

Radar altimeters transmit microwave pulses and measure echoes from the surface. Laser altimeters measure the travel time of optical pulses or individual photons. For a monostatic instrument the path is two-way. In the vacuum approximation the range is

\[R_{\rm obs}=\frac{c\Delta t}{2},\qquad h_w=H_{\rm sat}-R_{\rm corr}.\]

Here \(c\) is the speed of light, \(H_{\rm sat}\) is the satellite ellipsoidal altitude from precise orbit determination, and \(R_{\rm corr}\) is the range after instrument and propagation corrections. The simple height subtraction applies to near-nadir geometry; off-nadir measurements require full geolocation. GNSS, DORIS and satellite laser ranging contribute to orbit determination. The measured quantity is range, not water depth.

Sampling versus precision

Reference ocean missions such as Jason use approximately 10-day repeat orbits; Sentinel-3 uses a 27-day repeat orbit and SWOT's science orbit approximately 21 days. ICESat-2 has a nominal 91-day repeat cycle. Repeat period does not guarantee observations of every lake on each cycle. Ground-track spacing, footprint, cloud cover, ice, tracking mode and usable returns determine actual coverage. Precision, accuracy and spatial resolution are different quantities.

03 · Echo waveforms and retracking

A radar echo records returned power versus time or range gate. Retracking estimates the position of the leading edge, which determines range. Ocean-like diffuse returns, narrow specular returns from calm inland waters, and multi-peak returns contaminated by land require different models. A threshold crossing is a simple illustration, not an operational retracker.

\[\Delta R=\frac{c}{2}\Delta t_{\rm gate},\qquad \Delta h=-\Delta R.\]

Synthetic normalized echoes; the nominal gate is 0 ns. Threshold selection can cause range differences even for a stationary surface. A real retracker uses instrument-specific calibration, waveform sampling, noise and quality controls.

04 · From observations to a lake-level time series

Orbit & range→Retracking→Corrections→Water mask→Datum harmonization→Time series

Start with a documented Level-2 product or validated inland-water product. Select observations within an appropriate lake polygon and buffer away from contaminated shorelines where necessary. Apply waveform quality flags, geolocation checks, ice screening and outlier rejection. Aggregate individual observations to a pass-level estimate and uncertainty; retain actual observation dates.

\[R_{\rm corr}=R_{\rm obs}+\sum_i\delta R_i,\qquad h_w=H_{\rm sat}-R_{\rm corr}.\]

Here \(\delta R_i\) are signed additive range corrections. Product conventions differ: a field may represent a correction to range, a correction to height, or a delay to subtract. Read the metadata before applying any term. Typical considerations are dry/wet troposphere, ionosphere, instrument calibration, solid Earth tide, pole tide and ocean tidal loading. Ocean-specific sea-state bias, ocean tide and dynamic atmospheric corrections cannot automatically be transferred to inland lakes. Some terms are already applied in higher-level products.

For multi-mission merging, unify reference ellipsoid, vertical datum, tide system and correction conventions; estimate inter-mission offsets using overlap or independent gauges. Avoid treating all repeat-track samples as independent. Wind setup, river slope, waves, seiches and seasonal ice can make an observed surface nonuniform.

Combined height error
\[\delta h=\delta H_{\rm sat}-\delta R+\delta b.\]

Signed deterministic error illustration, not a statistical uncertainty calculation. Independent error variances would add; correlated terms require covariance.

05 · Vertical reference systems

Ellipsoid, geoid, orthometric height and depth

The reference ellipsoid is a mathematical surface realized through a terrestrial reference frame. The geoid is an equipotential surface of Earth's gravity field. Orthometric height is referenced to the geoid; geometric water depth is the vertical separation between water surface and lakebed. For the local height relationships used here,

\[\boxed{h=H+N},\qquad D=h_w-h_b.\]

The geocentric water-surface height \(h_w\) is not water depth \(D\). At a common horizontal location and epoch, subtracting the same geoid height from water and bed cancels in their difference. The exact orthometric-height definition involves gravity along the plumb line; the local geometric relation is the standard geodetic approximation.

07 · Time-variable geoid and gravity-field effects

Mass redistribution changes the gravitational potential, so the geoid can evolve. To first order at a fixed location, Bruns' formula relates disturbing potential to geoid height. Use consistent gravity, reference frame, tide system and potential convention.

\[\Delta N\simeq\frac{\Delta T}{\gamma},\qquad H_{\rm static}(t)=h_w(t)-N_0,\qquad H_{\rm dynamic}(t)=h_w(t)-N(t).\]\[\Delta H_{\rm static}=\Delta h_w,\qquad \Delta H_{\rm dynamic}=\Delta h_w-\Delta N.\]

A static geoid correction does not remove time-variable geoid effects. At a fixed horizontal location, a consistent geoid correction to both water surface and bed cancels in their geometric difference:

\[\Delta D=(\Delta h_w-\Delta N)-(u_b-\Delta N)=\Delta h_w-u_b.\]

Do not subtract geoid change twice. Nevertheless, the physical water surface follows an approximately equipotential surface in equilibrium; spatially varying gravity can redistribute water and change lake levels. A uniform reference change is not the same as a physical redistribution. Wind and hydrodynamic effects can depart from equilibrium. Changing ground tracks can also sample different static geoid heights, which must be harmonized before temporal comparisons.

08 · Lake volume and mass estimates

For a stable basin with area–height relation \(A(h)\), volume change is the integral of inundated area over height. The trapezoidal approximation is exact for a linear area–height relation and is not generally exact for arbitrary bathymetry.

\[\Delta V_{\rm static}=\int_{h_1}^{h_2}A(h)\,\mathrm dh\approx\frac{A_1+A_2}{2}(h_2-h_1).\]

For small perturbations of the bed and a common reference footprint, the first-order volume correction is the area integral of upward bed displacement. For a sloping, moving shoreline the first-order boundary term vanishes at zero depth; large changes require dynamic geometry. Horizontal deformation, sedimentation and density variations may require additional treatment.

\[\Delta V_{\rm corrected}\approx\Delta V_{\rm static}-\int_{S_0}u_b(\mathbf x)\,\mathrm dA,\qquad \Delta M_w\approx\rho_w\Delta V_{\rm corrected}.\]

Estimate lakebed motion from elastic and viscoelastic loading, tectonics and other relevant processes in a consistent frame. A GNSS station onshore is not necessarily representative of the entire lakebed. Use spatially resolved models and propagate uncertainty. In glacierized regions, such as Patagonia, solid-Earth rebound may bias long-term altimetric storage estimates.

09 · Sea-level budget and solid-Earth effects

Ocean altimetry measures geocentric sea-surface height. Relative sea level at a coast is sea-surface height minus local land motion. Water-column thickness is sea-surface height minus seafloor elevation. These are distinct quantities.

\[\Delta D_{\rm ocean}=\Delta SSH-u_{\rm floor},\qquad \Delta RSL=\Delta SSH-u_{\rm land}.\]

For a small-amplitude regional ocean budget, a schematic consistent-reference decomposition is \(\Delta SSH\approx\Delta h_{\rm mass}+\Delta h_{\rm steric}+u_{\rm floor}\). Mass and steric terms must describe column thickness under compatible conventions. GRACE gravity observations contain solid-Earth signals as well as water mass; GIA and earthquake gravity effects may require separate treatment. Do not double count deformation already included in a product. Geoid redistribution, ocean dynamics, reference-frame effects and moving boundaries must be treated consistently in a full sea-level equation.
